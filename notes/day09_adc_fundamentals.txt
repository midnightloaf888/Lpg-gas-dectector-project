Day 9 – ADC Fundamentals Study

Today I studied the fundamentals of the Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC) used in the ESP32. 
To build a strong understanding of the topic, I referred to multiple educational videos explaining how ADC works in both electronics and the ESP32.
I learned that many real-world sensors, such as gas sensors, temperature sensors, light sensors, and potentiometers, produce analog voltages rather than digital signals. 
Since a microcontroller can only process digital values, an ADC is required to convert these continuously varying voltages into digital numbers that the ESP32 can interpret.
I also understood the basic principle of analog-to-digital conversion.
The ADC samples the input voltage and converts it into a numerical value. On the ESP32, the ADC typically provides a 12-bit resolution, meaning the analog voltage can be represented by values ranging from 0 to 4095.
A voltege close to 0 V produces a value near 0, while a voltage close to the reference voltage produces a value near 4095.
During my study, I explored the ADC channels available on the ESP32 and learned the difference between ADC1 and ADC2.
I also came across practical considerations such as noise, fluctuations in analog readings, and why filtering or averaging is often used to obtain more stable sensor values.
I understood that the LPG gas sensor used in this project providesd ananalog output, making the ADC an essential part of the gas leak detection system.
 The ESP32 will continuously monitor these analog values and use them in later stages of the project to determine nrmal, warning,and alarm conditions.
